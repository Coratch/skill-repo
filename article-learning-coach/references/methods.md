# Methods

Use these methods as tools, not as rigid sections. Choose the smallest set that helps the user learn.

## Thesis-Claims-Evidence

Primary method for every article.

- Thesis: the article's central answer in one sentence.
- Problem: the question or tension the article responds to.
- Claims: 3-7 supporting assertions.
- Evidence: data, cases, citations, experiments, examples, or reasoning.
- Warrant: why the evidence supports the claim.
- Limits: scope, assumptions, missing evidence, and counterexamples.

Good diagnostic question: "If this article were wrong, which claim would fail first?"

## 5W2H

Use for first-pass orientation.

| Field | Reading question |
|---|---|
| Who | Who is writing, affected, cited, or assumed as reader? |
| What | What is the topic, object, mechanism, or recommendation? |
| When | What time period, trend stage, or sequence matters? |
| Where | What context, market, domain, system, or geography matters? |
| Why | Why does the problem matter? Why now? |
| How | How does the mechanism, method, or argument work? |
| How much | What magnitude, cost, frequency, benchmark, or impact is claimed? |

Do not force fields when the source has no evidence. Mark "not specified".

## SCQA

Use to reconstruct narrative logic.

- Situation: accepted background or starting context.
- Complication: tension, change, gap, contradiction, or failure.
- Question: the implied question created by the complication.
- Answer: the article's central answer.

If SCQA feels artificial, state that and use problem-answer-evidence instead.

## Claim-Evidence-Warrant

Use when the article is persuasive, strategic, scientific, or controversial.

| Part | What to extract |
|---|---|
| Claim | What the author wants the reader to accept |
| Evidence | What support the author provides |
| Warrant | The reasoning bridge from evidence to claim |
| Assumption | What must be true for the reasoning to work |
| Risk | Where the claim may fail |

Classify evidence strength as high, medium, low, or unsupported.

## Socratic Questioning

Use for deep understanding and critical reading.

- Clarification: What exactly does this term or claim mean?
- Evidence: What proves it? What would disprove it?
- Assumption: What is taken for granted?
- Boundary: Where does this apply or not apply?
- Alternative: What other explanation fits the evidence?
- Consequence: What changes if we accept this claim?
- Transfer: Where can the user apply this idea?

Ask 3-5 high-value questions, not a long questionnaire.

## Feynman Loop

Use in `coach` mode.

1. Ask the user to explain the thesis or concept in their own words.
2. Identify one most important gap, confusion, or unsupported leap.
3. Re-explain only that gap with a simpler example.
4. Ask the user to revise their explanation.
5. Repeat until the explanation is accurate, simple, and source-grounded.

Feedback format:

- Strong part
- Gap
- Correction
- Try again prompt

Do not answer everything for the user in coach mode. The learner must retrieve and explain.

## Memory Design

Prefer active recall over passive notes.

- First generate questions, then answers.
- Create flashcards for concepts, contrasts, mechanisms, numbers, evidence, and application rules.
- Avoid cards that only memorize prose.
- Use cloze deletion for definitions and mechanisms.
- Use scenario cards for transfer.

Review schedule default: today, tomorrow, 3 days later, 7 days later, 14 days later.

## Final Learning Check

A strong learning session should end with:

- One-sentence thesis
- Three core claims
- One strongest evidence
- One weakest assumption
- One useful application
- One question still worth investigating
